## ABSTRACT
Brain-Computer Interface technology translates mental activity into control commands, offering a vital communication channel for individuals with neuromuscular disorders. While Motor Imagery classification via Electroencephalogram signals and deep learning is highly effective, practical deployment is severely hindered by extreme inter-subject variability. This causes significant marginal distribution shifts, conventionally requiring lengthy 20-to-30-minute calibration sessions for new users. To address this calibration bottleneck, this research proposes the Euclidean Alignment Fine-Tuned (EA-FT) EEGNet framework, combining input-level standardization with weight-level adaptation.
The system takes raw, heterogeneous multi-channel EEG time-series data as inputs. The core process involves an unsupervised Euclidean Alignment module that mathematically normalizes spatial covariance across subjects, followed by processing through a pre-trained compact Convolutional Neural Network. During rapid transfer learning, early layers capturing universal neurophysiology are strictly frozen, while only the final user-specific classification layers are fine-tuned. The final outputs of the system are discrete motor intent predictions. The framework was rigorously evaluated using the BCI Competition IV 2a benchmark under a strict leakage-free protocol. ## INTRODUCTION
## 1.1 Introduction
Brain–Computer Interface (BCI) technology creates a direct communication pathway between the human brain and an external device, translating neural activity into control commands without relying on the body’s normal neuromuscular routes [2]. This capability is especially important for people with severe motor impairments, such as those caused by stroke, spinal cord injury, or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), who may be unable to use conventional assistive technologies. Among available neuroimaging modalities, electroencephalography (EEG) remains the most practical option for BCI research and deployment because it is non-invasive, relatively low-cost, portable, and suitable for real-time use.
EEG signals are the macroscopic electrical activity of the brain recorded from the scalp. At the cellular level, these signals arise mainly from the synchronized postsynaptic potentials of large populations of pyramidal neurons in the cerebral cortex [1]. When many of these neuron’s fire in a coordinated manner, weak extracellular currents form; after attenuation through brain tissue, skull, and scalp, the residual potentials appear at the surface as microvolt-range fluctuations. Because the signal must pass through several layers of tissue, scalp EEG has an inherently low signal-to-noise ratio and is easily contaminated by artifacts such as eye blinks (EOG) and muscle activity (EMG).
The raw EEG waveform is commonly decomposed into clinically and functionally meaningful frequency bands: delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (above 30 Hz) [14]. For motor-related BCIs, the most informative components lie in the sensorimotor rhythms, particularly the mu rhythm (approximately 7–13 Hz) and the beta band (approximately 13–30 Hz). These rhythms are strongly associated with motor planning and execution and form the neurophysiological basis of Motor Imagery (MI) decoding.
Several EEG-based control paradigms have been developed to turn brain activity into device commands. Steady-State Visually Evoked Potentials (SSVEP) are elicited when a user gazes at a flickering visual stimulus and produces oscillatory responses at the stimulus frequency and its harmonics. The P300 event-related potential is a positive deflection occurring about 300 ms after an attended rare (“oddball”) stimulus and is widely used in communication spellers. Hybrid BCIs combine two or more signal types to improve reliability and command diversity. In contrast, Motor Imagery is an endogenous paradigm: the user mentally rehearses a movement (for example, left hand, right hand, feet, or tongue) without physically executing it. Spatially, motor imagery activity is concentrated over the sensorimotor cortex, especially regions around the central sulcus. In practice, these signals are captured non-invasively using electrodes placed on the scalp according to the International 10–20 system, which standardizes electrode locations relative to underlying cortical areas [1]. A typical acquisition chain consists of electrodes (wet or dry), conductive media, high-gain amplifiers, filtering stages, and an analog-to-digital converter that digitizes the multi-channel time series for computer processing. Modern consumer and research headsets (for example, Emotiv and NeuroSky-type systems) have improved portability, but clinical and benchmark studies still commonly rely on denser electrode montages to preserve spatial resolution
Once acquired, EEG trials must be transformed into reliable motor-intent predictions. A conventional MI-BCI pipeline therefore proceeds through signal acquisition, preprocessing, feature learning or extraction, and classification. Compact convolutional neural networks such as EEGNet have proven effective for this task because they can learn discriminative temporal and spatial filters directly from multi-channel EEG [8]. However, practical deployment is hindered by extreme inter-subject variability. Differences in anatomy, skull conductivity, electrode placement, and cognitive baseline cause strong shifts in the statistical distribution of EEG across users. As a result, a classifier trained on one subject often fails on another, and new users are forced into lengthy 20–30-minute calibration
To address this calibration bottleneck, this thesis proposes the Euclidean Alignment Fine-Tuned (EA-FT) EEGNet framework. The end-to-end story of the proposed system can be summarized as follows. First, raw multi-channel EEG is acquired while the user performs (or imagines) motor tasks. Second, the signals are cleaned and band-limited to the MI-relevant 8–30 Hz range to emphasize mu/beta activity and suppress irrelevant noise. Third, unsupervised Euclidean Alignment normalizes spatial covariance across subjects so that heterogeneous EEG distributions are mapped into a more comparable input space. In this way, the research follows a complete flow from the biological origin of EEG, through motor-imagery generation and scalp-level capture, to alignment-aware transfer learning and few-shot prediction. The intended outcome is a more practical MI-BCI pipeline that preserves competitive decoding accuracy while substantially reducing the calibration burden for new users.

## 2.2.1 Early development of EEG-based BCIs
The foundational work for EEG can be traced back to Hans Berger in the 1920s, who successfully recorded weak electric currents from the human scalp and identified that brain activity patterns change according to functional status [1]. The concept of using brain signals to control external devices emerged in 1971, but the formalization of BCI technology truly began with pioneering studies in 1991. Research demonstrated that subjects could learn to control a computer cursor by voluntarily modulating their sensorimotor rhythms (SMRs), establishing a foundational proof-of-concept for operant conditioning in device control [6]. This early era relied heavily on traditional wet electrodes utilizing silver/silver-chloride (Ag/AgCl) sensors and conductive gels to reduce skin–electrode impedance bel
Figure 1 represents the International 10–20 electrode placement system used for standardized scalp EEG recording. The diagram shows the top view of the head with electrode positions labeled relative to anatomical landmarks (nasion and inion), including frontal (Fp1, Fp2, Fz, F3, F4, F7, F8), central (C3, Cz, C4), temporal (T3, T4, T5, T6), parietal (P3, Pz, P4), and occipital (O1, Oz, O2) sites. Distances between neighboring electrodes are defined as 10% or 20% of the nasion–inion or left–right reference measurements, which ensures consistent coverage of cortical regions. For motor imagery BCIs, electrodes over the sensorimotor cortex—especially C3, Cz, and C4—are particularly important because they capture mu and beta rhythm changes associated with imagined left- and right-hand movements.

In the present study, motor imagery decoding primarily relies on the mu/alpha and beta bands (approximately 8–30 Hz), because these rhythms show Event-Related Desynchronization (ERD) and Event-Related Synchronization (ERS) during imagined movement.
## 2.2.2 Recent developments and Future trends
The most significant recent development in BCI technology is the transition from manual signal processing to Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Deep Learning (DL) [4], [15]. Modern DL architecture enables end-to-end learning, where models accept raw or minimally pre-processed EEG data and automatically discover hierarchical features, eliminating the bottleneck of manual feature engineering [10], [13]. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), particularly the compact EEGNet, have revolutionized the field by successfully classifying multi-paradigm signals (MI, P300, SSVEP) while minimizing trainable parameters [17].
Simultaneously, hardware has evolved toward portable, wireless dry-electrode headsets (e.g., Emotiv, NeuroSky) that support rapid, self-administered deployment [10], [15]. Looking toward the future, the field is advancing toward Hybrid BCIs that combine multiple modalities (e.g., EEG with fNIRS or EOG) to maximize reliability [13], [16], as well as Shared Autonomy models where AI handles low-level execution (like a robotic grasp) while the human provides high-level intent [4].

## 2.3 Comparative Analysis of Existing Methods
Historically, BCI pipelines relied on traditional feature extraction algorithms combined with linear classifiers. While computationally light, these methods struggle to decode latent patterns in highly noisy environments. In contrast, Deep Learning approaches like CNNs provide robust spatial-temporal feature extraction, while Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) excel at tracking temporal dynamics for cognitive monitoring [8], [13].
Regarding the handling of inter-subject variability, Transfer Learning (TL) and Domain Adaptation (DA) have emerged as primary strategies [9], [15]. Deep Transfer Learning relies on pre-training a network on a massive source domain and fine-tuning the final layers for a new user, which has successfully reduced calibration times from 30 minutes to under 2 minutes [4], [9]. Alternatively, Manifold Learning utilizing Riemannian Geometry bypasses flat vector spaces entirely, mapping the covariance matrices of EEG signals onto a symmetric positive-definite (SPD) manifold [4], [13]. Classifiers operating on this manifold, such as Minimum Distance to Riemannian Mean (MDRM), show exceptional resistance to signal drift compared to traditional Euclidean methods [4], [14].

## 2.4 Strengths and Limitations of Current Approaches
Current deep learning approaches boast significant strengths. For instance, EEGNet's architecture utilizes depthwise and separable convolutions to remain highly parameter-efficient, making it ideal for real-time processing and minimizing the risk of overfitting on small datasets [17]. Transfer learning techniques are highly effective at leveraging existing datasets to bypass long initial training phases [10].
However, these approaches have notable limitations. Standard transfer learning can suffer from "catastrophic forgetting" if the marginal distribution shift between the source and target subjects is too extreme [3], [7]. DA techniques project features into a common subspace, but without strict input-level alignment, deep neural networks still struggle to unlearn the ingrained patterns of the source data [9]. Additionally, while dry and wireless electrodes drastically improve usability, their susceptibility to motion artifacts and higher impedance introduces a level of noise that current lightweight algorithms still struggle to filter in real-time [2], [6].

The most immediate practical barrier is the Calibration Bottleneck. For the primary target demographic patients suffering from severe neuromuscular disorders or motor impairments this extended setup induces significant cognitive and physical fatigue before the device can even be operated. ## 2.6 Discussion on Research Gaps
While extensive research has focused on complex network architectures (like heavy CNNs or RNNs) and sophisticated downstream domain adaptation, there is a distinct lack of focus on standardized input-space alignment prior to deep learning feature extraction [7], [8]. Methods like Riemannian Alignment address this but are computationally heavy and difficult to implement in real-time, low-latency BCI systems [3], [4], [7]. There is a gap in the literature regarding the synergistic combination of lightweight unsupervised input alignment with conservative weight-level network adaptation.
## 2.6.1 Definition of the Research Gap/Problem
The specific research gap is the lack of an efficient, unified framework that can standardize heterogeneous EEG input distributions across subjects before processing, thereby allowing a compact network to adapt to a new user rapidly without catastrophically forgetting universal brain rhythms [4], [7]. Without aligning the raw data space first, fine-tuning efforts remain data-hungry and inefficient.
## 2.7 Summary
This chapter explored the theoretical and historical landscape of EEG-based BCIs, highlighting the paradigm shift brought by deep learning and compact CNNs like EEGNet [17]. While AI has vastly improved decoding accuracy, the literature confirms that inter-subject variability and the resulting calibration bottleneck remain the most critical barriers to practical use [8], [9]. Analyzing existing transfer learning and domain adaptation methods reveals a distinct research gap: the need for an efficient, input-level standardization process to facilitate rapid, few-shot calibration [4]. Bridging this gap is the primary focus of the proposed methodology in the following chapters.
## CHAPTER 3
## THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS FOR THE EXTENSION
## 3.1 Introduction
This chapter establishes the theoretical groundwork required to address the high inter-subject variability inherent in Electroencephalogram (EEG) signals. It explores the foundational concepts of domain adaptation and transfer learning within the context of Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs). By critically analyzing the limitations of existing theories, this chapter provides a clear rationale for the proposed Euclidean Alignment Fine-Tuned EEGNet (EA-FT EEGNet) framework, detailing the mathematical and structural inspirations that drive this technological extension.
## 3.2 Overview of Existing Theory
The current theoretical paradigm for non-invasive BCI decoding relies heavily on detecting repeatable neurophysiological signatures and leveraging machine learning for pattern recognition.
Sensorimotor Rhythms (SMR) and Motor Imagery (MI): Motor Imagery theory relies on the voluntary modulation of sensorimotor rhythms. The mental rehearsal of a limb movement generates distinct neural patterns, characterized by Event-Related Desynchronization (ERD) within the mu (8–13 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) frequency bands.
Compact Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs): Modern BCI decoding has shifted toward optimized architectures like EEGNet. EEGNet utilizes depthwise convolutions to extract frequency-specific spatial filters and separable convolutions to decouple the relationships within and across feature maps. This reduces the trainable parameter count to approximately 2,000, making it vastly more efficient and less prone to overfitting than larger models like DeepConvNet, which possess over 170,000 parameters.
Transfer Learning (TL) and Domain Adaptation (DA): Standard TL theory involves leveraging data from a source domain (existing subjects) to initialize a model for a target domain (a new user). Domain adaptation aims to learn a target prediction function by minimizing the differences in marginal or conditional probability distributions between these domains.

Consequently, fine-tuning a network without first correcting this massive input-level discrepancy forces the deep learning model to drastically overwrite its weights. ## 3.4 Rationale for the Extension
To bridge this gap, intervention is required before the EEG data enters the neural network's feature extraction layers. The rationale for the proposed extension is that deep neural networks are highly sensitive to the scale and variance of their inputs. If heterogeneous subject distributions can be mathematically forced into a standardized space, the subsequent neural network will only need to learn the subtle, task-specific variations of the new user rather than entirely new spatial mappings. This necessitates an unsupervised domain adaptation technique that is computationally lightweight, combined with a highly compact CNN to prevent overfitting on the small datasets acquired during a rapid (< 3 minute) calibration phase [8].
## 3.5 Theoretical Foundations/Inspiration for Extension
The extension integrates two primary mathematical and architectural concepts to achieve domain invariance:
Euclidean Alignment (EA): EA is an unsupervised data whitening technique drawn from manifold learning. Instead of treating covariance matrices as points on a complex Riemannian manifold which is computationally heavy EA transforms the trials into Euclidean space so that their average covariance matrix equates to the identity matrix. ## 3.6 Characteristics of the Extension
The proposed EA-FT EEGNet extension is characterized by its dual-stage adaptation capability. It is highly parameter-efficient, utilizing the compact nature of EEGNet (~2,000 parameters) to ensure it can be trained on minimal calibration data without succumbing to overfitting [8]. The extension is also paradigm-agnostic, unsupervised at the alignment stage, and computationally inexpensive, making it highly suitable for real-time, low-latency deployment in clinical settings.
Computational Efficiency: Unlike Riemannian Alignment (RA) which requires complex mapping to and from symmetric positive-definite manifolds, EA is computationally lightweight because it operates entirely within Euclidean space.
Parameter Stability: By freezing Block 1, the framework maintains the integrity of general neurophysiological features, preventing overfitting on small calibration datasets.
Few-Shot Efficiency: The synergistic combination of EA and conservative fine-tuning targets a massive reduction in calibration data requirements, driving the setup time down from a traditional 20-30 minutes to under 3 minutes.
## 3.7 Bridging the research gap with extension
The extension bridges the research gap by standardizing the input space before attempting weight-level adaptation. By applying Euclidean Alignment, the marginal distribution shift is virtually eliminated, pushing the target user's data into the same statistical space as the source dataset. Once aligned, the frozen-layer fine-tuning strategy allows the network to adapt smoothly and conservatively. This completely bypasses the catastrophic forgetting phenomenon, ensuring that the model retains its fundamental understanding of motor imagery while accommodating the new user, drastically cutting the required calibration time.

## 4.3 Inputs and Outputs of Extension
Inputs: The framework accepts raw, multi-channel EEG time-series data represented as an ET matrix, where E represents the number of spatial electrode channels and T represents the number of discrete time samples recorded during a specific motor imagery task (e.g., 2 to 4 seconds of mental rehearsal).

## 4.4 Process workflow for Extension
The conceptual workflow of the EA-FT EEGNet extension is divided into three primary zones:

## 6.4.6 Deployable Euclidean Alignment (Zone 1, revised)
The Euclidean Alignment procedure described in section 6.4.4 estimates the reference covariance matrix from the complete set of trials belonging to a subject. While this is the formulation presented by He and Wu and is entirely adequate for offline analysis, it is transductive: the reference matrix is informed by trials that will subsequently be used to evaluate the model. A brain–computer interface operating in real time cannot behave in this way, because at the moment of calibration the trials the user will produce later in the session do not yet exist. Reporting accuracies obtained under a transductive alignment would therefore risk an unquantified degree of optimism. To eliminate this concern rather than merely acknowledge it, the alignment module was refactored so that both a transduc
The original routine, euclidean_alignment(X), was retained unchanged and performs the transductive computation. It accepts the full trial tensor of a subject, accumulates the second-moment matrix R̄ = (1/N)·Σ Xᵢ Xᵢᵀ across every trial, regularises it by the addition of 10⁻⁸ I to guarantee invertibility, derives the inverse square root R̄ through the symmetric eigen decomposition provided by numpy.linalg.eigh, and applies the resulting transformation to every trial by Einstein summation. Eigenvalues are floored at 10⁻¹⁰ before the negative-half-power is taken, which prevents a numerically degenerate channel from producing an unbounded scaling factor.
The second formulation decomposes the same mathematics into two independent operations, so that estimation and application no longer occur on the same data. The function ea_fit(X) performs only the estimation stage: it computes the mean second-moment matrix over whatever set of trials it is supplied with, applies the identical regularization and eigen decomposition, and returns the whitening matrix P = R̄^(−1/2) as a 22 × 22 array. The function ea_apply(P, X) performs only the application stage, projecting an arbitrary tensor of trials through a previously estimated and fixed whitening matrix by means of the expression np.einsum('cd,ndt -> nct', P, X). Because the two stages are separated, the matrix estimated at one point in time may be applied unchanged to data acquired at any later poin

This separation is what makes the deployable protocol possible. In that protocol, ea_fit is invoked once, on the thirty-six calibration trials alone. The matrix it returns is then frozen and applied by ea_apply to those same calibration trials and, without further modification, to every subsequent trial the subject produces. No test trial contributes to the estimate of the whitening transformation, and the procedure is causally realisable: it corresponds exactly to a live system that records a short calibration block on the user's arrival, computes a whitening matrix from it, and thereafter whitens each incoming trial as it is received. This transformation costs a single 22 × 22 matrix multiplication per trial and therefore imposes no measurable latency during operation.
Both formulations were carried out through the complete experimental pipeline. The transductive variant is used internally in the ablation study of section 7.7.7, where every experimental arm must share an identical alignment convention for the comparison between arms to be meaningful. The calibration-only variant provides the deployable result reported as the primary outcome of this research. Implementing both permitted the difference between them to be measured directly rather than assumed, and the analysis presented in section 7.7.6 establishes that the framework carries no transductive optimism whatsoever.

## 6.4.8 Implementation of the ablation arms
Establishing that the complete framework performs well would demonstrate only that the system works, not which of its components is responsible. Three additional experimental conditions were therefore implemented so that the contribution of each component could be isolated and attributed.
The first condition, implemented as train_from_scratch(), establishes the performance floor. A fresh EEGNet is instantiated with random weights and trained exclusively on the target subject's thirty-six calibration trials, with no transfer of knowledge from any other subject. Because no pre-trained representation exists to be protected, the learning rate is raised to 1 × 10⁻³, matching the pre-training configuration, and no layer is frozen. In every other respect the validation split, the early-stopping rule, the checkpoint criterion and the single-scoring of the test set the procedure is identical to fine_tune(), so that the comparison isolates the effect of transfer learning alone and is not confounded by differences in training protocol.
The second condition, zero-shot transfer, requires no additional training. The LOSO pre-trained source model is passed directly to the evaluation routine and scored on the target subjects held-out trials without any weight update whatsoever. This measures how much of the source population's knowledge transfers to a new individual in the complete absence of personalization.
The third condition, fine-tuning without Euclidean Alignment, is the control that isolates the alignment stage, and it required the most substantial additional computation. An entire parallel Leave-One-Subject-Out pre-training run was executed on dataset_noea, the band-pass-filtered but un-aligned counterpart of the aligned dataset, producing a second complete set of nine source models. Each of these was then adapted to its target subject using the same fine_tune() routine, the same learning rate, the same freezing strategy and the same early-stopping rule, differing from the proposed framework in one respect only: the absence of spatial whitening at the input.
A methodological safeguard binds these four conditions together and is essential to the validity of the statistical analysis in Chapter 7. For each target subject, the trial ordering is generated by a deterministic permutation seeded as RandomState(SEED + subject_id), and this identical permutation is regenerated inside every experimental arm rather than being drawn afresh. Consequently, the same thirty-six trials serve as calibration data and the same remaining trials serve as test data in all four conditions, for every subject. The four accuracy figures obtained for a given subject are therefore genuinely paired observations of the same underlying data, which is precisely the condition required for the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests reported in section 7.7.10 to be applicable.

No new human data were collected for this research, and no experiments were conducted on human participants by the author. An important consequence of the sample size should also be registered here, since it governs the interpretation of every statistical test in this chapter. ## 7.6 Evaluation Metrics
Four quantitative metrics were used to characterize classification performance, supported by one geometric metric that assesses the alignment stage in isolation and one operational metric that captures the practical objective of the research.
Classification accuracy is the proportion of test trials assigned to the correct class. Because the binary dataset is almost perfectly balanced (Table 3), accuracy is not distorted by class prior and can be interpreted directly against the 50 % chance level. It is the primary metric because it is the metric universally reported in motor-imagery literature, which makes external comparison possible.
Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) measures the probability that a randomly chosen right-hand trial receives a higher SoftMax score for the right-hand class than a randomly chosen left-hand trial. It is reported alongside accuracy because it is threshold-independent: accuracy summarizes the classifier at the single operating point produced by a 0.5 decision threshold, whereas AUC summarizes the quality of the underlying ranking across all possible thresholds. In a BCI intended for real deployment this distinction matters, because the decision threshold can be tuned after the fact to trade false activations against missed intentions, and AUC indicates how much room there is to perform that tuning.
Cohen's kappa (κ) corrects the observed agreement between prediction and ground truth for the agreement expected by chance, according to   where
is observed agreement and is chance agreement. It is the metric mandated by the BCI Competition IV evaluation protocol itself, and it is included so that the results reported here can be placed directly alongside competition entries. A value of 0 indicates performance indistinguishable from guessing; conventional interpretation regards 0.21–0.40 as fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 as moderate, 0.61–0.80 as substantial and above 0.80 as almost perfect.
The confusion matrix decomposes accuracy into its constituent error types, showing separately how often left-hand imagery is misread as right-hand and vice versa. It is reported per subject because a classifier that is accurate overall but systematically biased towards one class would be unusable as a two-command interface, and only the confusion matrix exposes such a bias.
Frobenius distance from the identity matrix is the geometric metric used to assess the alignment stage independently of any classifier. For a subject whose mean spatial covariance matrix is , the quantity   measures how far that subject's spatial statistics deviate from the standardized, perfectly white reference state that Euclidean Alignment is designed to impose. Comparing this distance before and after alignment tests directly whether the alignment operation does what the mathematics says it should and comparing the spread of the distance across subjects’ tests to see whether inter-subject heterogeneity has been removed.
Calibration duration is the operational metric that encodes the practical objective of the research. It is computed as the number of calibration trials multiplied by the measured acquisition cadence of 8.03 seconds per trial, divided by 60. It is emphasized that this is derived from the median spacing between consecutive trial onsets in the raw recordings, and therefore accounts for the fixation, cue and rest periods of the Graz protocol as well as the imagery window. Reporting calibration time as 36 × 4 s = 2.4 minutes, on the basis of the analysis window alone, would understate the true burden on the user by a factor of two, and this thesis deliberately reports the larger, honest figure.

## 7.7.6 Deployment-realistic alignment: eliminating transductive optimism
An important methodological question arises from the way Euclidean Alignment was applied in sections 7.7.1 to 7.7.5. In that pipeline the reference covariance matrix  was estimated over every trial belonging to the target subject, including the trials that were subsequently used for testing. This is a transductive procedure. It is common in alignment literature, and it is not fraudulent, but it is not deployable: a live BCI performing calibration at nine o'clock in the morning cannot incorporate the statistics of trials the user will produce at eleven. Any published accuracy obtained this way carries an unknown amount of optimism, and the honest course is to measure that optimism rather than to assume it is negligible.
The alignment was therefore re-run in the form a real system would use. The whitening matrix was estimated from the 36 calibration trials alone, using `ea_fit`, and that single fixed matrix was then applied unchanged to both the calibration trials and every test trial, using `ea_apply`. Fine-tuning and scoring then proceeded exactly as before. Table 8 compares the two.
Table 8: Transductive versus deployment-realistic (calibration-only) Euclidean Alignment
The result is both reassuring and, at first sight, counter intuitive. Estimating the alignment reference from only 36 trials does not degrade performance; it very slightly improves it, by 0.6 percentage points on average. Four subjects improve, one degrades marginally, and four are entirely unchanged.
There are two reasons for this. The first is that the estimate of a 22 × 22 covariance matrix converges quickly: 36 trials of 1,000 samples each supply 36,000 samples per channel, which is far more than sufficient to characterize the second-order spatial structure of a 22-channel montage. Adding a further hundred trials refine an estimate that is already effectively converged. The second reason is more interesting. Because a within-session EEG recording drifts over time as electrode impedance changes and the subject fatigues, a reference estimated from the early trials the calibration block is a better match for the statistics the model was fine-tuned on than a reference averaged across the entire session. The practical consequence is significant and should be stated clearly: the framework carries no transductive optimism whatsoever, and the deployable accuracy of 74.1 % is therefore adopted as the headline result of this research. A model with so few degrees of freedom, updated so cautiously, extracts almost all of the information it is capable of extracting from a very small number of examples, and then stops improving. What appears at first to be a disappointing insensitivity to data is in fact the intended behavior of a system engineered to be data-efficient, and it converts directly into the practical claim this research sets out to make.
That claim can now be stated with precision. At the 36-trial protocol adopted throughout this chapter, calibration lasts 4.82 minutes, representing a reduction of 75.9 % against a twenty-minute conventional session and 83.9 % against a thirty-minute one, and comfortably meeting the five-minute plug-and-play threshold. More strikingly, the data show that the protocol could be reduced to eighteen trials, or 2.41 minutes, at a cost of only 0.5 percentage points of accuracy (73.2 % against 73.7 %). At that operating point the reduction against a conventional session reaches 88.0–92.0 %, which satisfies the "approximately 90 %" target stated in the abstract of this thesis. ## 7.7.10 Statistical significance
Every difference reported above was tested for statistical significance. The unit of analysis is the subject, giving nine paired observations per comparison, and each baseline is compared against EA-FT on the identical held-out trials, so the pairing is exact. A two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used in preference to a paired t-test because nine observations are far too few to justify an assumption of normality and because the per-subject accuracies are visibly non-normal, exhibiting the tri-modal structure noted throughout. The matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation r is reported as the effect size, where a value near ±1 indicates a highly consistent effect in one direction.
Table 12: Wilcoxon signed-rank tests of EA-FT EEGNet against each baseline (n = 9 subjects, two-sided)
The statistical picture is unambiguous and should be read carefully.
The comparison against the from-scratch baseline is significant at p = 0.0039 with a perfect effect size of r = +1.000, meaning that every one of the nine subjects, without a single exception, is better served by the transfer-learning framework than by training on their own calibration data alone. The median advantage is 16.98 percentage points. It is worth noting that p = 0.0039 is the smallest two-sided p-value attainable with nine paired observations; this result is as strong as this sample size permits any result to be. The value of the transfer-learning architecture is therefore established beyond reasonable doubt within this dataset.
Neither of the other two comparisons approaches significance. The difference between EA-FT and zero-shot transfer (p = 0.575) provides no evidence that fine-tuning changes performance; the median difference is exactly zero. The difference between EA-FT and fine-tuning without alignment (p = 0.426, r = −0.311) provides no evidence that alignment changes accuracy in either direction, and in particular the apparent 2.4-point disadvantage of the aligned pipeline noted in section 7.7.7 is not statistically supported and should not be treated as a real effect
Two caveats govern the interpretation of the non-significant results, and both must be stated. First, with nine subjects the test has low statistical power, and a true effect of a few percentage points would very likely go undetected. A non-significant result here means "no effect was demonstrated", which is not the same as "no effect exists". Second, and conversely, it would be equally improper to invoke low power as a way of preserving the original hypothesis: the honest summary is that this experiment, at this sample size, does not demonstrate an accuracy benefit from Euclidean Alignment, while it does demonstrate through the variance and worst-case analyses of sections 7.7.7 and 7.7.8 a benefit in robustness that a mean-accuracy test is not designed to detect.

## 7.10 Summary
This chapter presented a complete evaluation of the EA-FT EEGNet framework on the BCI Competition IV 2a benchmark, under a strict leakage-free protocol in which held-out test trials are scored exactly once and all model selection is driven by data carved out of the calibration set.
The alignment stage was verified to perform its intended geometric function: Euclidean Alignment reduces the Frobenius distance of each subject's mean covariance matrix from the identity by 99.0 % on average and eliminates inter-subject heterogeneity entirely, collapsing a 5.14-fold spread across subjects to a single common value, at a computational cost of 150 ms per subject.
The complete framework attains a deployable mean accuracy of 74.1 %, a mean AUC of 0.809 and a mean Cohen's kappa of 0.471 across nine subjects after a calibration session of 4.82 minutes, representing a reduction in calibration burden of 75.9 % to 83.9 % against conventional twenty- to thirty-minute protocols. A supplementary analysis showed that the protocol can be shortened to 2.41 minutes for a cost of 0.5 percentage points, taking the reduction to 88–92 %. The reported figures carry no transductive optimism: estimating the alignment reference from calibration trials alone was shown to match and marginally exceed the transductive estimate.
The ablation study attributed this performance to its components. Transfer of the pre-trained representation is decisive and statistically significant, outperforming a from-scratch baseline by a median of 16.98 percentage points for all nine subjects without exception (p = 0.0039, r = 1.000). Conservative fine-tuning adds 0.8 points on average and, more importantly, is shown to be safe: no subject suffers catastrophic forgetting. Euclidean Alignment does not produce a significant accuracy improvement in this experiment +0.5 points at the zero-shot transfer stage and −2.4 points after fine-tuning, neither significant but it reduces the across-subject standard deviation from 15.7 to 12.4 points and rescues the weakest transferrers, improving the three worst-performing subjects by up to 13.0 
Against published work, the framework matches EEGNet with alignment-based methods [21], [22] to within 0.1 percentage points while requiring one-third of the calibration time and exceeds all non-transfer deep learning baselines by 8.3 to 11.7 points while requiring one-sixth of their calibration time. The dominant source of variance throughout the evaluation is not any algorithmic choice but inter-subject variability, which spans 33 percentage points and exceeds every measured algorithmic effect by an order of magnitude. Chapter 8 draws these findings together into the conclusions of the research and sets out the further work they motivate.

## 8.5 Limitations of the Study
Six limitations bound the conclusions of this research, and they are stated without mitigation.
The sample size cannot support the inferences the field routinely draws from it. Nine subjects are the standard size of this benchmark, but it is the subject and not the trial that constitutes the unit of analysis for a claim about cross-subject generalization, so the effective sample size is nine regardless of the 1,292 trials involved. A two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on nine pairs cannot return a p-value below approximately 0.004 even for a perfectly consistent effect, and with a between-subject standard deviation of 12 to 16 percentage points the study has adequate power only for effects exceedingly roughly ten points. Consequently, the non-significant results in Table 12 should be read as "no effect demonstrated" rather than "no effect exists", and no confident claim can be made 
The task is binary, whereas the framework was designed to be paradigm-agnostic. All results concern left- versus right-hand imagery. This is the most strongly lateralized class pair in the motor-imagery repertoire and therefore the pair for which a spatial-alignment method should perform best. Extension to the four-class problem including foot and tongue imagery, whose cortical representations are medial and substantially overlapping, would test the framework under materially harder conditions, and nothing in this evaluation predicts how it would fare.
The evaluation rests on a single dataset and a single recording setup. All nine subjects were recorded at one institution, on one amplifier, with one electrode montage, at one sampling rate, under one experimental protocol. The framework's behavior under a different montage, a different sampling rate, dry rather than wet electrodes, or a consumer-grade amplifier is unknown. Euclidean Alignment is in principle the component best suited to absorbing such differences, but that proposition is untested here.
The participants were healthy, while the intended beneficiaries are not. The nine subjects had no neurological or motor impairment. Stroke, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and spinal cord injury are all associated with cortical reorganization that alters the topography, amplitude and reliability of sensorimotor rhythms. Neither the alignment behavior nor the transfer behaviour measured here can be assumed to be carried over to a clinically impaired population, and this work is therefore an algorithmic feasibility study rather than clinical validation.
The evaluation is offline, on pre-recorded and pre-segmented trials. Every trial was cue-locked, correctly labelled and cleanly windowed. A live system must additionally detect when the user intends to issue a command at all, cope with signal drift across a session, tolerate electrode movement, and contend with the closed-loop dynamics by which a user's imagery changes in response to the feedback the system provides. None of these factors is present in an offline benchmark, and offline accuracy is known to overstate online performance.
Artifact removal was approximated rather than implemented as specified. The design in Chapter 5 calls for Independent Component Analysis with the ADJUST plugin. Because a full ICA-ADJUST decomposition requires an offline MATLAB/EEGLAB stage that could not be integrated into the automated Python pipeline, a variance-based peak-to-peak rejection at a 5σ threshold was substituted. This removes gross contamination it discarded 0.5 % of trials but it does not decompose and selectively removes ocular and muscular components in the way ICA-ADJUST would, and residual artifact may therefore be present in the retained data.
## 8.6 Challenges Encountered
Four difficulties shaped the course of this research and are recorded here because each altered the reported results.
Discovering and eliminating test-set leakage. The first working version of the pipeline selected the best fine-tuning epoch by monitoring accuracy on the held-out test set. This is a subtle but consequential form of leakage: the reported figure becomes the accuracy of the best of thirty checkpoints as judged on the test data, which is not an accuracy any deployed system can achieve. Identifying the problem required tracing why the reported numbers seemed inconsistent with the flatness of the fine-tuning curves. The remedy was to carve a 25 % validation split out of the calibration trials and drive all selection from it, scoring the test trials exactly once. Every result in the thesis was regenerated under the corrected protocol. The reported accuracy fell, and the work is materially strong
Establishing an honest measure of calibration time. The initial calculation multiplied 36 trials by the four-second analysis window, giving 2.4 minutes. Inspection of the raw `.mat` structures revealed that each cued trial occupies a complete protocol cycle of fixation, cue, imagery and rest. Extracting the per-run trial-onset indices and taking the median spacing across 2,538 intervals gave the true cadence of 8.03 seconds per trial, doubling the honest figure to 4.82 minutes. Reporting the smaller number would have been defensible only by a definition of "calibration time" that no user would recognize, and the larger figure is reported throughout.
Confronting a result that contradicted the hypothesis. The ablation showed that fine-tuning without alignment reached 75.8 % against the proposed framework's 73.4 %, directly opposing the central claim of Chapter 4. The temptation to tune the aligned pipeline until it won was real and was resisted; doing so would have amounted to selecting a hyper-parameter based on the comparison it was meant to adjudicate. The response instead was to design a cleaner experiment that removed fine-tuning entirely and isolated alignment at the transfer stage, and then to examine the per-subject pattern rather than the mean. That analysis revealed the systematic structure reported in section 7.7.8 alignment rescues failing transferrers and mildly penalizes successful ones which produced a more interesting an
Parsing the raw dataset and reconciling documentation with structure. The BCI Competition IV 2a `.mat` files use a nested MATLAB struct array in which trial onsets, labels, sampling rate and artifact flags are distributed across per-run structures, some of which contain EOG calibration runs with no motor-imagery trials at all. Loading required `struct_as_record=False` with `squeeze_me=True` and careful handling of the resulting inconsistent array dimensionalities, together with explicit skipping of runs without trials, one-based to zero-based label remapping, and validation that every extracted segment had the expected shape of 22 × 1000 before being retained. Reconciling the documented structure with the actual file contents consumed a disproportionate share of the implementation effort.
## 8.7 Future Work
Seven directions follow from the findings and the limitations above, ordered by the strength of the case for pursuing them.
Scale the source domain and re-test the alignment hypothesis The most direct explanation for why Euclidean Alignment did not improve mean accuracy here is that eight source subjects are too few for a population-level standardization to matter, and that EEGNet's depthwise convolution learns its own spatial compensation when the source domain is small. This is directly testable. Pre-training on a large, aggregated corpus the MOABB collection, PhysioNet, or the 54-subject OpenBMI dataset [5] and repeating the identical ablation would determine whether the benefit of alignment grows with source diversity. This is the highest-value next experiment because it addresses the central open question of the thesis rather than an adjacent one.
Increase the number of subjects until the statistical tests have adequate power. Nine subjects cannot resolve effects below ten percentage points. Repeating the evaluation across forty or more subjects, drawn from multiple datasets, would permit the differences of two to three points reported in Table 9 to be resolved rather than merely observed, and would allow the robustness benefit identified in section 7.7.8 to be tested directly by comparing the variance of the two conditions rather than only their means.
Adopt subject-adaptive rather than uniform protocols. The dominant finding of Chapter 7 is that inter-subject variability exceeds every algorithmic effect by an order of magnitude, and that alignment helps weak transferrers while mildly harming strong ones. This suggests abandoning a single fixed protocol. A practical scheme would estimate the whitening matrix from a very short calibration block, measure the covariance distance between the new user and the source population, and then select the pipeline accordingly: apply alignment and extended calibration for users who are distant from the source distribution, and bypass alignment with minimal calibration for users who are close to it. This converts the per-subject pattern discovered in this research directly into a deployable decision ru
Extend to the four-class problem and to a rejection option. Four-class decoding including foot and tongue imagery is the natural next step in capability. It should be accompanied by an asynchronous formulation with a rejection class, since the ROC analysis of section 7.7.5 showed that the framework's confidence ordering is substantially more reliable than its decisions at a fixed threshold. Permitting the system to abstain on low-confidence trials rather than forcing a decision would convert that reliable ordering into usable accuracy, at the cost of throughput a trade-off likely to be favorable for assistive control.
Validate across recording hardware. The framework should be evaluated on datasets recorded with different montages, sampling rates and amplifier characteristics, including dry-electrode consumer devices. This is the scenario in which Euclidean Alignment has the strongest theoretical case, since hardware differences manifest precisely as differences in spatial covariance scale, and it would test whether the robustness benefit identified here extends from inter-subject to inter-device variability.
Move from offline analysis to closed-loop online operation. Every result in this thesis was obtained on pre-recorded, cue-locked and correctly labelled trials. Connecting the pipeline to a Lab Streaming Layer input from a live amplifier such as an OpenBCI Cyton board would permit measurement of true end-to-end latency, of performance degradation across a session as electrode impedance drifts, and of the closed-loop effects by which feedback alters a user's imagery. Online accuracy is expected to be lower than the offline figures reported here and quantifying that gap is necessary before any clinical claim can be made.
Evaluate with the clinically impaired population. The final step, requiring institutional ethical approval and clinical collaboration, is evaluation with stroke survivors or individuals with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Cortical reorganization in these populations may increase the distance between a new user's spatial statistics and those of a healthy source population, which is precisely the regime in which section 7.7.8 found alignment to be most beneficial. There is therefore a specific, testable reason to expect that Euclidean Alignment may prove more valuable for the intended clinical population than it did for the healthy subjects evaluated here and confirming or refuting that expectation is the single most consequential open question left by this thesis.
